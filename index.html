Blog Post 1

How Do We Know Truth?

By Chelsea Thea E. Salvacion & Samantha Anne Y. Pomida · Grade 12 - Hestia · Final Output: The Bloggers

Knowing the truth is one of the most difficult parts of being human because we do not always see reality clearly. We have limited information, personal biases, emotions, and experiences that can affect our judgment. For me, knowing truth begins with asking a simple question: What evidence supports this claim?

Evidence can come from observation, reliable records, repeated testing, logical reasoning, and trustworthy testimony. For example, if someone tells me that it will rain tomorrow, I should not treat the statement as a fact just because the person sounds confident. I can check a reliable weather forecast, observe the conditions, and compare information from dependable sources. The more independently supported evidence a claim has, the more reasonable it is to accept.

Truth-seeking requires both confidence to make judgments and humility to change them when better evidence appears.

Philosophical discussions about knowledge also remind us that simply believing something does not guarantee that it is true. Plato's Theaetetus (Plato, 1997) explores the difficult relationship among knowledge, perception, and belief. In everyday life, this distinction is important. A rumor can be believed by hundreds of people and still be false.

Our Physics class gave us a real example of why truth-seeking matters. We had a debate about cheating, especially because many students acknowledged that cheating happens and that a majority of students may do it. This raised an important question for us: Does something become right simply because many people do it? We realized that the fact that cheating is common is different from the opinion that cheating is acceptable. The debate pushed us to separate what we know from what we assume and to ask what evidence or reasoning supports a claim.

I also think truth requires intellectual honesty. If new evidence proves that I was wrong, admitting the mistake is not a weakness. It is part of learning. A person who refuses to reconsider an idea, even after strong evidence appears, may be protecting a belief instead of seeking truth.

Therefore, we know truth as responsibly as possible by combining evidence, reason, careful observation, credible sources, and openness to correction. We may not achieve perfect certainty about everything, but we can improve the quality of what we believe by taking truth seriously.

References

Plato. (1997). Theaetetus (M. J. Levett, Trans.; M. Burnyeat, Rev.). Hackett Publishing Company. (Original work published ca. 369 BCE)

Blog Post 2

How Is Truth Different From Opinion?

By Chelsea Thea E. Salvacion & Samantha Anne Y. Pomida · Grade 12 - Hestia · Final Output: The Bloggers

Truth and opinion can sound similar because both can be expressed as statements. The important difference is that truth is concerned with whether a claim accurately describes reality, while an opinion expresses a person's judgment, preference, interpretation, or belief.

Consider the statements, "Water freezes at 0°C under standard atmospheric pressure" and "Chocolate ice cream is the best flavor." The first is a factual claim that can be tested under specified conditions. The second is a personal preference. We can disagree about the second statement without either person being factually wrong.

However, opinions are not always completely disconnected from evidence. Someone might say, "I think this school policy is effective because attendance improved after it was introduced." The conclusion is still an opinion, but it can be supported or challenged by evidence. This is why good discussion should distinguish between what I feel and what the evidence shows.

Our Physics debate about cheating showed this distinction clearly. The statement “many students cheat” is a factual claim that can be investigated. But “cheating is acceptable because most students do it” is a judgment. A behavior being common tells us something about what people do, but it does not automatically prove that the behavior is fair, correct, or justified.

Social media makes this distinction especially important. A post can have thousands of likes and still contain inaccurate information. Popularity tells us what many people think; it does not automatically establish what is true. Likewise, confidence, repetition, or a person's status does not turn an unsupported claim into a fact.

In my view, respecting opinions does not mean treating every opinion as equally accurate. People deserve respect even when their claims are mistaken. We can listen, ask for reasons, check evidence, and disagree without turning the conversation into a personal attack.

Truth and opinion can therefore coexist, but they serve different purposes. Opinions help us express values and preferences, while truth gives us a standard for evaluating claims about reality. Learning to tell the difference makes our conversations more thoughtful and responsible.

References

Russell, B. (1912). The problems of philosophy. Williams and Norgate.

Blog Post 3

Is Truth Always Superior to an Opinion?

By Chelsea Thea E. Salvacion & Samantha Anne Y. Pomida · Grade 12 - Hestia · Final Output: The Bloggers

At first, it seems obvious that truth should always be superior to opinion. If we are trying to understand reality, a well-supported truth is more dependable than an unsupported belief. Yet the question becomes more interesting when we consider what people mean by "superior."

When a question has an objective answer, truth should guide us. For example, if a bridge has a serious structural problem, an engineer's evidence-based assessment should matter more than someone's personal opinion that the bridge "looks safe." In situations involving health, safety, science, or important decisions, replacing evidence with preference can have serious consequences.

But many human questions involve values and personal experiences. People can reasonably disagree about which hobby is most enjoyable, which music is most moving, or which school activity is most meaningful. These judgments do not always have one measurable answer. In such cases, an opinion can be appropriate even though it is not an objective fact.

The goal is not to eliminate opinions; it is to know when an opinion needs evidence and when it expresses a value or preference.

The same Physics debate showed that truth and opinion can answer different kinds of questions. We could discuss the factual question of whether cheating was common, while still disagreeing about the ethical question of whether it should be accepted. Some students focused on how widespread the behavior was, while others focused on honesty, fairness, and the purpose of learning. Recognizing that difference made the debate more thoughtful.

Truth can also be communicated poorly. A person may know something true but present it arrogantly or without considering another person's perspective. Good thinking therefore needs more than factual accuracy. It also needs humility, empathy, and judgment. Being correct does not give someone permission to disrespect others.

I would say truth is superior to opinion when the two are competing as explanations of reality. However, truth does not make every personal preference meaningless. A mature thinker knows the difference between facts that should be checked and values that people may reasonably debate.

References

James, W. (1897). The will to believe. The New World, 5, 327–347.

Blog Post 4

What Would the World Be Like If There Were No Truth, Only Opinion?

By Chelsea Thea E. Salvacion & Samantha Anne Y. Pomida · Grade 12 - Hestia · Final Output: The Bloggers

Imagine waking up in a world where there is no truth, only opinion. People could say that a historical event happened or did not happen, that a scientific experiment succeeded or failed, or that a promise was kept or broken—and every statement would be treated as equally valid. At first, this might sound like complete freedom. In practice, it would make everyday life extremely difficult.

Communication depends on shared reality. If there were no way to distinguish accurate information from false information, it would be difficult to make reliable plans. A student could not depend on a schedule, a community could not confidently respond to an emergency, and researchers could not build new knowledge from previous discoveries.

Justice would also be affected. Courts and communities need evidence to determine what happened. If every claim were automatically treated as equally true, there would be no meaningful way to investigate conflicting accounts. Decisions could become based only on power, popularity, or emotion.

Science would face an even bigger problem. Scientific knowledge depends on observation, testing, evidence, criticism, and the possibility that a conclusion can be revised. If an unsupported opinion had exactly the same status as a carefully tested explanation, there would be no reason to conduct experiments or correct mistakes.

Most importantly, relationships depend on trust. Friends, families, teachers, and communities need some confidence that words can refer to real events. A world with no truth would make trust fragile because we could never know whether another person's claims had any connection to reality.

Our classroom discussion also showed how difficult things become when popularity is treated as the only standard. If everyone simply accepted cheating because “everyone does it,” then common behavior would replace shared standards of honesty and fairness. A classroom needs more than a majority opinion; it needs reliable expectations that allow students to learn and trust one another.

This thought experiment shows why truth is not merely an academic idea. Truth helps organize knowledge, communication, justice, science, and relationships. Opinions are valuable because they allow people to express different perspectives, but a functioning society needs shared standards for deciding which claims are supported by reality.

References

Russell, B. (1912). The problems of philosophy. Williams and Norgate.

About Us

Why Truthfully Thinking?

Truthfully Thinking is a student-created blog exploring how people decide what to believe. The blog focuses on truth and opinion because these ideas appear in everyday life—from classroom discussions and social media to science, friendships, and important decisions.

One real classroom experience that inspired this project was a Physics debate about cheating. Because many students recognized that cheating happens and that a majority of students may participate in it, the discussion made us question whether something being common makes it true, fair, or acceptable. That experience became a useful starting point for our reflections on truth and opinion.

Our goal is not to pretend that every question has an easy answer. Instead, we want to practice asking better questions, examining evidence, recognizing different perspectives, and changing our minds when good reasons require it.
